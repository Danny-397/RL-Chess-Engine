A chess engine that learns from its own games

An AlphaZero-style engine written from scratch in Python and PyTorch, and a controlled look at why its self-play training stalls.

Three opponents: the trained network alone, the network inside a small search, and a classical alpha-beta engine.

What the experiments show

Within three iterations, self-play collapses into threefold repetition: the network learns that every position is a draw, so the search has no reason to convert an advantage. Ten times more self-play does not escape it. Blending a material count into the search keeps decisive games in the data. Measured against the network each run started from, only that arm got measurably stronger, by about 31 Elo, from a single training seed: a modest result, reported with its uncertainty.

How it is built

Network
A residual CNN with a policy head over AlphaZero's 4,672-move encoding and a value head. 4 blocks, 64 channels.
Search
PUCT Monte-Carlo tree search, with the value head at the leaves instead of rollouts.
Training
Self-play games become (position, visit distribution, result) examples for a combined policy and value loss.
Second engine
A classical alpha-beta searcher with quiescence and piece-square tables, rated about 1390 against strength-limited Stockfish.
Tests
50 tests in CI, aimed at the bugs that fail silently: board mirroring, value sign flips, move-encoding round trips.

What I found

Self-play training stalled in a recognisable way. These are the measurements, what they support, and what they do not.

The draw cycle

The engine could win material but not convert it. In the baseline run, the untrained network scored a few accidental checkmates in its first two iterations. From iteration 3 onward, all 80 self-play games ended in threefold repetition.

The mechanism is in the value target. When every game is drawn, the outcome z is 0 for every training position, so the value head learns that every position is even. A search guided by that value has no reason to prefer a won position over a repetition, so the next batch of games is drawn too. The value loss falling to about 0.01 is a symptom of this, not a sign of good training. The policy loss falling from 4.5 to 0.8 means less than it appears to as well: the targets are the search's own visit counts, which sharpen as the network does.

A confound, and a control

Arm B was evaluated with the same search it trained with, so its moves against the random opponent were also guided by the material heuristic. Its Elo therefore mixes two things: what the network learned, and what the heuristic does on its own. The original write-up attributed all of it to learning.

To separate them, I evaluated freshly initialised networks that had never been trained, under the same settings. A rule that ended games early

The engine stopped a game as soon as a draw could be claimed. In python-chess a repetition claim is valid when the side to move merely has a move that would repeat a position a third time, so games ended as “threefold repetition” before anyone repeated, and the search scored every such position as a dead draw, even for the side that was winning. Every number above used that rule, so the comparisons between them hold, but the draw counts are inflated and the rule itself pushes towards draws.

The rule is now the real one: three actual occurrences, or a fifty-move counter that has reached 100. Under it, the network on this site still ends all six of its recorded self-play games in a genuine threefold repetition, after 25–36 plies (the replay above). How much of the July draw rate the rule caused is one of the things the multi-seed rerun measures.

A sturdier yardstick

Most games against a random mover are drawn, which is why an untrained network can land anywhere from about −7 to +120 against it. The repository now has a second opponent: one that mates when it can and otherwise grabs the most material, looking no further. Untrained networks lose most games to it outright:

What the data supports

  • The draw cycle is real. From iteration 3 the baseline's self-play is all threefold repetition, and ten times the games leaves 88% of them undecided.
  • Neither unassisted arm measurably changed strength. Both intervals include zero; the earlier reading that the baseline lost strength came from comparing it with the wrong untrained network.
  • The assisted arm improved a little. About +31 Elo over its own starting network, just clear of zero, from one training seed. Treat it as a lead, not a result.
  • The draw rule mattered. Games ended on a merely available repetition. The behaviour is genuine (real repetitions under the corrected rule), but the July draw counts are inflated by an amount still to be measured.
  • The original headline does not hold. “Signal, not compute: −21 → +28” compared networks evaluated with different searches, with no starting-network reference and no intervals.

Limitations

  • One training seed per arm, and untrained networks alone span over 100 Elo across seeds. Differences between arms are within what a second seed could move.
  • The GPU run's numbers are transcribed from its captured output; its raw logs and checkpoints were lost with the session. The transcription is in one JSON file, and the figure is rebuilt from it.
  • Random is a weak yardstick. It separates a broken engine from a working one, not a good engine from a better one.
  • The network you can play on this site is an early checkpoint from a short CPU run, not one of the GPU-trained networks.

What would settle it

Three seeds per arm under the corrected draw rule; every network judged against random (with and without the material blend) and against the greedy opponent, next to its own starting network; the value probe on every network; and one run under the old rule to size its effect. That is one Kaggle notebook, about nine GPU hours.

How it works

The three parts of AlphaZero (a network, a search and a self-play loop), implemented from scratch. python-chess supplies only the rules.

The network

A position becomes an 18 × 8 × 8 tensor: twelve piece planes, four castling planes, en passant and the fifty-move counter. It is always drawn from the side to move, so the network never has to learn the game twice, once per colour. A small residual CNN (4 blocks × 64 channels) maps it to two outputs:

  • a policy: logits over all 64 × 73 = 4,672 moves in AlphaZero's encoding (56 sliding moves, 8 knight moves and 9 under-promotions per square), masked to the legal ones;
  • a value in [−1, 1]: the expected result for the side to move.

The search

Each move runs a fixed number of simulations down a tree. At every node the search picks the child that maximises

Q(s,a) + cpuct · P(s,a) · √N(s) / (1 + N(s,a))

The first term is the average value found below that move. The second is the network's prior, discounted as the move gets visited. Leaves are scored by the value head, not by random playouts, and each value flips sign as it is backed up a ply. The visit counts at the root are the search's verdict and, during training, its teaching signal.

Self-play and training

  1. Play a game against itself, choosing moves by search, with Dirichlet noise at the root and sampling for the first 15 plies.
  2. Store every position with the root visit distribution π, then fill in the result z when the game ends.
  3. Train on a replay buffer of recent positions, minimising
L = (z − v)² − π · log p + λ‖θ‖²

A better network makes a better search, whose games make better targets. That loop is the whole method; the Findings page shows what happens when its value target degenerates.

Which engine you are playing

Network
The value head alone, one ply deep, run in your browser with ONNX Runtime. It is an early checkpoint from a short CPU run, so it plays weakly.
Hybrid
A material and capture search in your browser. The network's value only breaks near-ties (worth at most 0.4 pawns), so this mostly plays like the search.
Classical
Alpha-beta with quiescence, move ordering, piece-square tables and an endgame term, on the server. At depth 2 it scored 14–8–8 against Stockfish limited to UCI_Elo 1320: about 1390 (95% CI 1284–1512) on Stockfish's scale, not a FIDE rating.

Before the browser trusts the network, it checks its own board encoding against golden values exported from Python. A mismatch would feed the network garbage without any error.
